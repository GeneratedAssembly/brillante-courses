Optics is old. The Greek geometers — Euclid and Hero among them — already
worked with a *ray model* around 300 BCE: light travels in straight lines, and
vision problems (why your reflection in a mirror, why a stick bends in water)
could be attacked with nothing but geometry. What they got wrong was the
direction of causation — several Greek thinkers, including Euclid himself and
later Ptolemy, took vision to work by rays *emitted from the eye* rather than
received by it.

The decisive experimental turn came from the Islamic Golden Age. Working in
Cairo around 1021 CE, **Ibn al-Haytham** (Alhazen) wrote the *Book of Optics*,
arguing — with experiments involving mirrors, lenses, and the camera obscura —
that light comes *from objects into the eye*. He also stated a quantitative
version of the law of reflection and studied refraction with air–water
interfaces. For the first time, optics was an experimental science.

### From laws to equations

The two founding laws of ray optics were pinned down in Europe:

- **Reflection**: the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence —
  known to the Greeks, restated quantitatively by Ibn al-Haytham, and used
  routinely by lens and mirror grinders.
- **Refraction**: light bends when it crosses into another medium. The
  quantitative rule, now called **Snell's law**, was discovered by Willebrord
  Snellius around 1621 (earlier, and more cryptically, by Thomas Harriot and
  Ibn Sahl in the 10th century).

### What *is* light? A 200-year argument

With the laws in hand, the question moved from *how light bends* to *what
light is made of*. Two camps formed:

- **Isaac Newton** (1670s–1704, *Opticks*) favored a **corpuscular** picture:
  light as a stream of tiny particles. Rays traveling in straight lines felt
  particle-like, and Newton's prestige kept the corpuscular view dominant for
  a century.
- **Christiaan Huygens** (1678, *Traité de la Lumière*) proposed that light is
  a **wave**, with every point on a wavefront acting as a source of secondary
  wavelets. His principle explains reflection and refraction *and* predicts a
  phenomenon particles cannot easily explain: light bending around edges —
  **diffraction**.

The wave camp won in two famous steps:

1. **Thomas Young (1801)** — the double-slit experiment produced bright and
   dark *fringes*, the signature of waves interfering. Newton's particles had
   no mechanism for darkness.
2. **Augustin-Jean Fresnel (1810s)** — a full wave theory of diffraction,
   whose quantitative predictions (bright spot in the shadow of a disk! — the
   Arago spot) survived the French Academy of Sciences' contest jury,
   which included committed corpuscularists Siméon Poisson and Pierre-Simon
   Laplace.

### Light as electromagnetism — and beyond

In 1865, **James Clerk Maxwell** derived that electromagnetic disturbances
propagate at a speed computable from static electricity and magnetism
experiments — and it matched the measured speed of light to within error.
Light *is* an electromagnetic wave. This is the turning point where this
course switches frameworks in [the wave optics foundations](20-rays-to-waves.md)
section: geometric optics stops being a set of rules and becomes an
approximation to Maxwell's equations.

The story does not end there. Around 1900–1905, blackbody radiation and the
photoelectric effect forced **Einstein's photon** picture: light exchanges
energy in quanta. Modern optics lives with both descriptions — waves for
propagation and imaging, photons for detection and quantum limits. This course
stays with the wave picture, which is the right one for everything from lenses
to lasers to resolution limits.
